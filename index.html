About Me

My background is in algorithms, graph theory, and algebraic combinatorics, which I studied at the University of Waterloo.

My first research project explored how to frame learning problems arising in combinatorial optimization. Rather than training a model to solve an entire problem, I used the theory underlying an approximation algorithm to identify the states in which its decisions could be suboptimal, then trained a graph neural network to replace those decisions selectively. For the Steiner tree problem with edge lengths 1 and 2, I proved a classification of the states of a classical heuristic into those where its decisions are guaranteed to be optimal and those where they may not be. While the project involved substantial model training and benchmarking, I found myself most drawn to the mathematical analysis and proofs. This made me realize that I want to pursue research in which developing mathematical theory and proving theorems are the primary objectives, rather than using theory mainly to guide computational experiments.

Around the same time, I found myself drawn back to linear algebra, particularly its rich theoretical structure and the depth of questions it raises. I wanted to explore these questions in a setting that remained closely connected to algorithms. Randomized numerical linear algebra (RandNLA) offers this combination, using tools from linear algebra and probability to develop efficient algorithms for large-scale matrix problems with rigorous guarantees on their accuracy. I am currently studying sketching, subspace embeddings, and randomized low-rank approximation, and plan to pursue graduate study in this area.
